Compares Esri, MapTiler and IGN satellite tiles of the same place, flags differences that are unusual against a baseline measured on ordinary locations (blurring, mosaicking, mismatched imagery), and shows every finding with its evidence tiles.

The problem

Sensitive sites are often hidden in commercial satellite imagery, but every provider does it differently: one blurs, one mosaics, one shows a capture from years ago. Someone looking at a single map never notices. Putting sources side by side by hand is slow and misses the real question: is the difference between two images actually unusual, or the ordinary kind seen everywhere (capture date, season, colour balance)?

What it does

  • Scan: downloads the same z16 tile from every applicable source (Esri, MapTiler, IGN in France) for a coordinate, queued on Celery.
  • Two signals: image difference between source pairs (SSIM, pixel, histogram) and whether one source is markedly blurrier than the rest (Laplacian, FFT, a regional blur map, five rules).
  • Score against a baseline: raw measurements are mapped to 0–100 against the distribution measured on ordinary locations; 50 means the 99th percentile of ordinary. The UI draws that line next to every score.
  • Evidence: tiles side by side (nearest-neighbour, so mosaics are not smoothed), reasons with their score contributions (+15, +10 → raw score 25), and a slider overlay comparison.
  • Verification: the community makes the final call; users who vote accurately gain trust.
  • Disabled signals are stated openly: the building detector and historical change are not hidden when they do not work; the UI explains why they are off.

Measurements

The struck-through value is what was found, the one beside it what was measured after the fix. Every number comes from the project’s own record.

How it was built with AI

The first version (April 2026) was built from a project plan with AI agents in two days, five phases: map, scan queue, image analysis, verification, CI, Docker. Everything was there and the tests were green. The September 2026 audit with Claude Code asked one question: what does this system actually measure? The answer was uncomfortable:

  1. 1The thresholds had never been measured. The “anomaly” threshold was a hard-coded 15% difference, and 39 of 40 ordinary locations exceeded it. The system flagged everything. The fix was a calibration script: run ordinary locations through the same pipeline and derive thresholds from p90/p99.
  2. 2Alignment was corrupting the evidence. Tiles of different resolutions were centre-cropped, and in one place OpenStreetMap’s drawn map was being “compared” with a satellite photo.
  3. 3The data source was indefensible. Imagery came from unofficial endpoints with a random browser identity, a terms-of-service violation in a public repository. It now uses official sources (Esri, MapTiler, IGN).
  4. 4The demo data was made up. Central London had a hand-written 91.5% “hidden structure” score. Demo data now comes only from the real engine’s reference scan.
  5. 5A general-purpose model cannot recognise buildings from above. The “ghost building” analysis relied on a COCO model and would have produced fabricated results. It was switched off, and the UI says why.

Every behaviour change was pinned with a regression test (230 tests).

Design

A night-shift analysis desk: a dark full-screen map, glass panels at the edges, very little colour. Four category colours for categories only, red for errors only. The score has no “good/bad” colour, because it is not a probability. Coordinates, scores and record IDs are monospace, and source attribution sits beside every tile.

Takeaway

AI can build a system in two days, but trusting what it says requires a human to demand the measurement. The audit’s most valuable output was not a feature but a baseline distribution.

Honest limits

What this project does not do, or has not proven yet.

  • No live deployment; the staging workflow is triggered by hand.
  • The building detector is off (it needs weights trained on aerial imagery). The historical-change analyser exists but is not wired into scans.
  • Calibration used a small sample (40 + 20 locations, z16); p99 is in practice close to the maximum.
  • A difference alone does not prove a facility exists; the differences at Avord and Volkel may come from capture dates. Findings are for human verification.
  • The map page scores 54 for mobile Lighthouse performance: the shell loads client-side together with the WebGL map.
